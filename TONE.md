## Voice Attributes

The voice stays consistent across channels. Only its length, structure, and level of detail change.

### 1. Evidence-led, never inflated

**Definition:** Describe work through specific scope, decisions, and outcomes that can be supported by public evidence.

**Sounds like:** “At FPT Software, I contribute to four backend services in a 13-service supply-chain simulation with a three-person Scrum team.”

**Does not sound like:** “I transformed a complex enterprise ecosystem with world-class engineering.”

**Do:**

- Name the system, team context, and personal contribution when they are verified.
- Separate personal work from team outcomes.
- Link case-study claims to code, documentation, or a live product when available.

**Don't:**

- Claim ownership of a whole system when the work was collaborative.
- Invent impact metrics, users, scale, or business results.
- Use superlatives in place of evidence.

**Anti-example from past content:**

> “Good ideas. Well-built software.”

### 2. Clear, never simplistic

**Definition:** Make the point easy to scan while keeping the technical detail that explains the work.

**Sounds like:** “TanStack Query manages remote API data; Redux Toolkit handles interface workflows.”

**Does not sound like:** “I leverage a robust, next-generation technology stack to deliver seamless solutions.”

**Do:**

- Put the main point in the first sentence.
- Explain technical choices in terms of responsibility, trade-offs, or behavior.
- Use familiar words around necessary terms such as NestJS, PostgreSQL, Kafka, and gRPC.

**Don't:**

- Stack technologies without saying what they do in the project.
- Explain basic concepts to an engineering audience unnecessarily.
- Use jargon as a substitute for a clear sentence.

**Anti-example from past content:**

> “Keep questioning. Keep learning.”

### 3. Calmly confident, never boastful

**Definition:** State experience directly and let the work carry credibility.

**Sounds like:** “I build full-stack products across interfaces, APIs, and data, using TypeScript, React, NestJS, and PostgreSQL.”

**Does not sound like:** “I’m an exceptional engineer who can solve any challenge.”

**Do:**

- Use first person for personal contribution and ownership.
- Use concrete verbs such as build, contribute, design, test, and maintain.
- Acknowledge a project's limits or trade-offs when relevant.

**Don't:**

- Add unsupported claims about seniority or expertise.
- Hide behind passive voice when describing personal work.
- Overstate a learning project as a production business result.

**Anti-example from past content:**

> “Learning by doing. Growing with every project.”

### 4. Product-minded, never vague

**Definition:** Connect engineering choices to the people, workflows, or system behavior they support.

**Sounds like:** “The outbox keeps an order update and its event in one local transaction; consumers still need idempotency because delivery can repeat.”

**Does not sound like:** “I care about creating impactful digital experiences.”

**Do:**

- Explain the problem before the implementation.
- Show how interfaces, APIs, data, and operations fit together.
- Include a meaningful trade-off instead of presenting every choice as perfect.

**Don't:**

- Describe a project only as a feature list.
- Use “user-focused” or “end-to-end” without explaining what that means.
- Present a technical choice as universally best when it depends on context.

**Anti-example from past content:**

> “A broader engineering perspective.”

## Lexicon

### Preferred terms

| Term | Use for | Don't confuse with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| contribute / tham gia, đóng góp | Work done as part of a team | Own / sở hữu toàn bộ |
| backend service / dịch vụ backend | A separately scoped service in a system | Entire platform / toàn bộ nền tảng |
| trade-off / đánh đổi | A cost or constraint of a technical choice | A defect by default |
| workflow / quy trình | A connected user or system process | A list of unrelated features |
| full-stack product / sản phẩm full-stack | Work spanning UI, API, and data | Equal depth in every layer |
| evidence / bằng chứng | Public code, tests, architecture, or shipped behavior | An unverified claim |

### Banned terms

| Banned | Use instead | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “rockstar developer” | “Software Engineer” plus relevant evidence | Inflated recruiting cliché |
| “passionate about technology” without a concrete example | Name the problem or work being explored | Generic and difficult to verify |
| “world-class”, “revolutionary”, “cutting-edge” | Describe the actual design or capability | Unsupported superlative |
| “leverage” as a synonym for use | use, build with, apply | Corporate/AI cliché |
| “seamless” without a measurable or observable explanation | Describe the user flow or system behavior | Vague product claim |
| “expert in” without strong, verifiable basis | Name the technology and demonstrated scope | Overstates proficiency |
| “end-to-end” without naming the boundaries | Say which layers or workflow are included | Empty scope phrase |
| “results-driven” | State the result | Generic self-description |

## Channel-Specific Guidance

### Portfolio website

**Hard constraints:** Page titles and descriptions must match each page; keep headings semantic; ensure Vietnamese and English versions are separately reviewed. Preserve the existing route and metadata conventions.

**Tonal shift from voice baseline:** Give enough context for a recruiter to scan, then offer technical depth in the case study.

**Do:**

- Put the Software Engineer role, product breadth, and location or work context near the opening.
- Use project pages to explain problem, individual contribution, decisions, and trade-offs.
- Link claims to available code, tests, architecture, or live demos.

**Don't:**

- Repeat the same keyword list in every section.
- Hide team context or blur personal contribution.
- Add technical claims to structured data that are absent from visible content.

**Example opening sentence in this channel:** “I’m a Software Engineer at FPT Software, contributing to backend services while building full-stack products across interfaces, APIs, and data.”

### LinkedIn

**Hard constraints:** Keep the first lines clear before truncation; use short paragraphs that scan on mobile; check current platform limits before publishing.

**Tonal shift from voice baseline:** Slightly more conversational than the CV; lead with a concrete engineering observation or verified contribution.

**Do:**

- Open with a specific detail, decision, or lesson.
- Use one short paragraph per idea and include a link to deeper evidence.
- Keep employer and team details within public disclosure boundaries.

**Don't:**

- Start with an engagement-bait question.
- Turn every update into a personal victory story.
- Use long hashtag lists, emoji stacks, or unsupported performance claims.

**Example opening sentence in this channel:** “I work across software product development, from React interfaces and APIs to data workflows and engineering delivery.”

### CV

**Hard constraints:** Keep the summary and bullets concise, factual, role-relevant, and consistent with the portfolio; use a readable PDF and standard section labels.

**Tonal shift from voice baseline:** Most compressed and formal channel; remove conversational transitions while retaining concrete scope.

**Do:**

- Start bullets with direct action verbs and state the scope.
- Separate employment, team projects, and personal projects clearly.
- Tailor the order of skills and evidence to the role without changing facts.

**Don't:**

- Use first-person pronouns in every bullet.
- Claim ownership of team results.
- Add hidden or repeated keywords for applicant-tracking systems.

**Example opening sentence in this channel:** “Software Engineer building full-stack products with TypeScript, React, NestJS, and PostgreSQL.”

## Global Do's and Don'ts

### Do

1. Name the work before describing the personality behind it.
2. State personal contribution separately from team scope.
3. Keep one core positioning across Vietnamese and English.
4. Localise each version naturally instead of translating word for word.
5. Use specific engineering nouns and active verbs.
6. Explain one meaningful trade-off in technical case studies.
7. Keep the first paragraph understandable to a recruiter.
8. Link public claims to proof where possible.
9. Keep dates, role, education status, and technologies consistent across channels.
10. Ask before publishing employer-sensitive or unverified information.

### Don't

1. Do not invent metrics, outcomes, users, scale, or responsibilities.
2. Do not imply that a team achievement was solely personal work.
3. Do not call a project production-scale unless the evidence supports it.
4. Do not list technologies without context.
5. Do not repeat broad claims such as “well-built software” without proof.
6. Do not use recruiter clichés or self-awarded superlatives.
7. Do not confuse interest with expertise.
8. Do not over-explain standard concepts in recruiter-facing copy.
9. Do not publish confidential names, data, or implementation details.
10. Do not prioritise keyword repetition over clear, useful writing.
